We landed in Tokyo at 10pm, exhausted from the flight but too wired to sleep. The hotel in Nihonbashi was a about a 1 hour metro ride away, and I remember thinking how quiet the train was. Nobody talking. Just the hum of the carriage and the soft chime at each station. We made sure to get some cash and snacks from a convenience store on the walk to the hotel from the metro station.

The next morning started with Tsukiji fish market. The alarm was set for 6:30am, which felt criminal, but the jet lag made it bearable. The outer market is packed with vendors selling the freshest fish, tamagoyaki, and seafood skewers you can find. We walked to Ginza afterward, past the Seiko clocktower, and spent a ridiculous amount of time in the Uniqlo flagship store. Twelve floors. Twelve. We were expecting to buy a lot, but nowhere near as much as we did. Uniqlo in Japan was way more cost effective than the ones we have in the U.K, spent enough to qualify for the free tote bag. 

<img src="/tsukiji.jpeg" class="image fit">

Akihabara was overwhelming in the best way. Arcades stacked five floors high, electronics shops with gadgets I have never seen, and vending machines on every corner. I picked up a pair of Sony WH-1000XM6 headphones at Yodobashi Akiba, which saved me a couple hundred pounds compared to UK prices. If you are into tech, budget time here.

The rhythm of Tokyo started to make sense by day three. Wake up early, hit the temples and shrines before the crowds, wander through whatever neighbourhood caught my eye, eat constantly. Asakusa was exactly what I hoped for: Sensoji Temple in the morning light, melon pan from a street vendor, and a walk along the Sumida River with the Tokyo Skytree looming in the distance. We booked an Omakase Tempura lunch with the Amex platinum pocket concierge service, I'm not going to lie, it was the best food we ate. For anyone interested, the restaurant was called Tempura Shimomura, they also had a halal friendly menu. 

<img src="/sensoji.jpeg" class="image fit">

Shibuya was chaos, but the kind I wanted. The scramble crossing lived up to its reputation. We stood on the pedestrian bridge outside the station and watched wave after wave of people cross in every direction. It is hypnotic.

A practical note: if you are Muslim and looking for halal food, Japan has more options than I expected. Gyumon in Shibuya does halal wagyu ramen, and it was pretty good ramen. We did end up trying to try as many different kinds of food besides ramen though.

On day six, we took a bus from Akihabara to Kawaguchiko to see Mount Fuji. The bus ride was about two hours, and I recommend picking up breakfast from a 7-Eleven beforehand (luckily there was one opposite the bus stop). Japanese convenience stores are on another level. Onigiri, egg sandwiches, hot coffee. All excellent, all well priced.

<img src="/fuji.jpeg" class="image fit">

Fuji was not clear that day, apparently it is only visible for 28 days of the year, or something crazy like that. We rented a car and drove around Lake Kawaguchi, stopping at Shiraito no Taki Waterfalls, and Makaino farms, which I definitely recommend if you want to try some amazingly creamy soft-serve ice cream. Japanese soft serve is something else by the way, DO NOT SKIP IT. On our second day in Kawaguchiko we went to Arakurayama Sengen Park for the famous pagoda view.

After grabbing some snacks for the shinkansen and some light stress finding the correct bus stop to get to Mishima, the next leg took us to Kyoto via bullet train. I had sent our luggage ahead using a forwarding service, which meant I could travel light. Highly recommend this. For about £25, your bags arrive at your next hotel before you do. I also recommend apple tagging your bags.

Kyoto felt different from Tokyo. Busy, it seems like it is a destination for everyone including the locals. The streets in Higashiyama were seriously busy, and walking up to Kiyomizu-dera Temple took longer with all the crowds, I recommend a really early start for tourist heavy spots. The wooden stage overlooks the city, and you can understand why people have been making this climb for centuries.

<img src="/kiyomizu.jpeg" class="image fit">

Fushimi Inari was quieter in the evening, and it started to rain, but it was still worth it. The thousands of vermilion torii gates wind up the mountain, and if you keep walking past the main trail, the crowds thin out. We walked the full loop, which took about 90 minutes, and found we were alone for stretches of it.

<img src="/senbon-tori.jpeg" class="image fit">

The Arashiyama bamboo forest is smaller than photos make it look. I will be honest. with the amount of people there, it still delivered. The soft-serve we had afterwards was perfect. Different cities seem to have special flavours that aren't available anywhere else, if only I knew this before choosing the chocolate vanilla mix, I would have gone for the roasted soybean flavour...

TeamLab Biovortex was unlike anything I have experienced. I had booked tickets weeks in advance, and I am glad I did. The installations blur the line between digital art and physical space. You walk through, and the art responds to your presence. It felt like stepping into someone's dream.

From Kyoto, we took the Shinkansen to Hiroshima. The Peace Memorial Park is sobering. The Atomic Bomb Dome stands exactly as it was left, a skeleton of a building surrounded by a city that rebuilt itself around it. I spent a long time at the Children's Peace Monument, reading the paper cranes left by visitors. It puts things in perspective. There also happened to be a festival going on, so we caught some live music and dances. I'm still searching for some of the songs for my playlist. Tried some okonomiyaki for dinner, and it is actually much tastier than you would expect for a dish with a lot of cabbage. I'm thinking I should have bought one the cabbage shavers so that I could make my own. 

The next morning, we caught an early ferry to Miyajima to see the floating torii gate. It was low tide when we arrived, so we walked out to the gate itself and stood where water would be in a few hours. The hike up Mount Misen was steep even after going most of the way on the rope-way, but the view from the top made up for the burning legs.

<img src="/miyajima.jpeg" class="image fit">

Osaka was our final stop. The energy here reminded me of Tokyo, but with its own personality. Dotonbori at night, with the neon signs reflected in the canal, felt like a movie set. We took a cruise along the Tombori River, which gave a different view of all those signs and the crowds lining the waterfront.

Nara was a day trip from Osaka, and yes, the deer are everywhere. They bow for treats. They also have zero personal boundaries. Watch your pockets. Todaiji Temple houses an enormous bronze Buddha, and the scale of the wooden hall that contains it is staggering.

<img src="/nara-deer.jpeg" class="image fit">

On our last morning, we had fluffy pancakes at a place called Happy Pancake, which took itself very seriously. The pancakes arrived at the table jiggly and golden, and I understood the hype. As a recommendation, try the black sesame flavour (if it is still available). A fitting end before the airport limousine bus to Kansai International.

<img src="/fuwa-pancake.jpeg" class="image fit">

Two weeks felt like a month, in the best way. Japan has a way of slowing you down even when you are walking 25,000 steps a day. The trains run on time, the food is exceptional, and everywhere you look there is something small and thoughtfully designed.

I am already planning the next trip. This time with 2 large empty suitcases.
