Every travel journal I’ve owned has gone the same way. Page one gets a proper entry with full sentences and everything. Page two is shorter. By page three I’m writing the date and “good day”, and by the time I’m home the thing is two-thirds empty and living in a drawer, quietly judging me.

For years I assumed I was just bad at journaling. I don’t think that’s it anymore. “Write about your day” is a genuinely hard prompt. A blank page is intimidating even if you like writing, and at the end of a day where you’ve done 20,000 steps in the heat, working out what’s worth recording is the last thing you want to do.

What fixed it for me was giving up on the diary entry entirely and answering small questions instead.

## Small questions beat big ones

“Write about today” asks you to build a narrative out of nothing. “What did you eat that you’d order again?” asks for one line. You can manage the second one half asleep with your shoes off, and a page of small specific answers usually tells the story better anyway, because the specifics are the first thing you lose. I can still describe the chocolate cake at Landeau in Lisbon five years on. I couldn’t tell you much else about that afternoon.

So here’s the list I use instead of “how was your day”.

1. **The date and where you were.** It feels too obvious to bother writing down. Write it anyway. In five years “that little café” means nothing, but “the café near Notre-Dame, 14th June” brings the whole morning back.
2. **One thing you ate that you’d order again.** Food memories fade quickly and they’re some of the best ones. You don’t need a review, just the dish and whether it’s worth chasing down again.
3. **A word or phrase you picked up.** It doesn’t need to be complicated. “Grazie mille” or “arigatō” will do. These are oddly the details that bring a whole trip back years later.
4. **Where you got lost.** The wrong turns are where the stories live. The planned parts blur together, but getting lost in Trastevere for an hour is the bit you’ll actually tell people about.
5. **Someone you talked to.** A shopkeeper, a stranger on a train, another traveller. Not their life story, just who they were and what you talked about.
6. **What surprised you.** Anything that didn’t match what you expected. A city that felt nothing like its reputation, weather, prices, a food that wasn’t what you thought it was.
7. **What you’d skip next time.** Not everything is a highlight, and that’s genuinely useful for the next trip, or for whoever asks you for recommendations six months later.
8. **Cities and regions, ticked off.** A plain running list of where you actually went, kept separate from the writing. Almost no effort and very satisfying to look back on.
9. **A rating out of five.** It forces a gut-check verdict you can compare across a whole trip without writing another word.
10. **One photo with one caption.** Not a gallery. One photo, one line about why you took it. The constraint makes you pick a favourite instead of dumping fifty into a folder you’ll never open again.

None of these need good writing. That’s the point. The barrier was never whether you can write, it was standing in a hotel room at 11pm deciding what to write about. Once the categories are already there, filling them in takes two minutes on a bad day.

## The version I built

This is the idea behind My Travel Scrapbook, an interactive PDF journal built around these prompts instead of blank pages. Every country gets its own two-page spread with the categories already in place: favourite food, a word you learned, cities explored as tap-to-check trackers, photo slots, and a notes page for the longer stuff. There’s never a blank page looking back at you, just boxes to fill.
